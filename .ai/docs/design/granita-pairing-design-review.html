Call 1  ·  CAMERA OR WORDS

Camera first, and the words are not a footnote — they are the other button

Recommendation. Keep an entry screen, and put both credentials on it as full-width buttons, 50pt each, camera above. The camera is ordered first, not privileged: it is one motion when the Mac is in front of you, and it is the only path that carries the spki (see §6, which is the real reason it leads). The words sit directly beneath it, same width, same weight, same height. No “or enter a code” link, because a link is a confession that one of the two is for people who failed.

Rejected: opening the camera immediately. This is the option I wanted, and it does not survive the sequence. The reader taps a Mac in discovery before going to the Mac; at that instant there is no code on screen — the QR only exists after “Pair a device” is chosen. A viewfinder that opens onto a desktop with nothing to find is a screen that says nothing while asking for the one thing the reader has not done yet. So the entry screen's real job is not the choice. It is the sentence “open Granita in your Mac's menu bar and choose Pair a device” — the only instruction in the flow that concerns the other machine, and there is nowhere else to put it.

Also rejected: words first. Your Screens case is real and recurring, but it is recurring for you: one reader who develops the app, on the two devices that make the camera impossible. Ordering the six words above the camera for everyone taxes the ordinary pair — a Mac two feet away — to spare the unusual one, and the unusual one is already spared by a button of equal size that is never more than one tap away. If the same-device case turns out to be common in TestFlight, the fix is not a reorder: it is remembering the last credential this phone used and putting that one on top.

On the already-paired state, and the Bonjour field. Draw it, then design it away. The record change is worth making — but the payoff is not this screen, it is the row in discovery: a paired Mac's row can go straight to its worktrees, and then the only readers who ever see “Already paired” are the ones whose token the Mac has revoked. That is the state worth having, and it is why the second button is plain and not bordered: re-pairing is a repair, not an option. Two costs I would take alongside it: the token store must remember a pairedAt date, or the sentence loses “on 12 August” and gets duller; and the row must not claim “Paired” from a TXT record alone once §6 is settled.

Should feel like the Apple TV remote pairing screen. It does not ask you to choose a method. It tells you what to do on the other machine, and then it waits without hurrying you.

3.2 a  /  b  ·  PERMISSION

A refusal is a preference, so stop treating it as a fault

Recommendation for the pre-alert state. Draw the screen the alert lands on, because the reader reads it while deciding. It holds the qrcode.viewfinder symbol, one line — “Waiting for camera access” — and, already, the six-words button. Whichever way they answer, the screen behind the alert had the answer on it. And the alert's own message is NSCameraUsageDescription, which is copy we write and the one string in this flow that has to earn a tap: it names what is read, and says nothing is photographed or stored.

Recommendation for the refusal. The primary action is Enter the Six Words, and the description does not mention Settings at all: “You do not need it.” Nothing has gone wrong — the reader declined a camera and there is a second credential built precisely for that. Settings drops to a plain button underneath, named for what it does rather than where it goes: Turn the Camera On in Settings. It stays because a reader who declined by reflex should be able to change their mind in one tap; it is demoted because it leaves the app, and leaving the app to fix a state that has an in-app remedy is the wrong first suggestion.

Rejected. A ContentUnavailableView whose only action opens Settings — that is the shape this state usually ships in, and it is a dead end that happens to have a button. Also rejected: pre-flighting the permission on the entry screen so the alert appears before the camera is chosen. It asks for a capability the reader may never use, and it burns the one prompt iOS gives you at the moment it is least explicable.

Should feel like being offered the stairs when the lift is out — not being told the lift is out.

3.2 e  ·  found, and being spent
Call 3  ·  PRESENTATION, AND WHAT THE VIEWFINDER OWES

A pushed screen, because the two credentials have to be siblings

Recommendation. Push it, in the NavigationStack discovery already has. Four screens, one spine: Macs → this Mac → Scan or Words → the result. The scanner and the six-word field are then siblings at the same depth, and moving between them is a back tap and a second tap — a reader who cannot scan does not have to dismiss anything, and no state is ever behind a state.

Rejected: a sheet. Detents are for content you consult while the screen underneath still matters. Here it does not, and the two things a sheet is good at both work against us: it can be dragged away mid-spend, and it leaves the entry screen visible behind a viewfinder, which reads as though the camera were a widget on that screen rather than the step after it. On the six-word screen the objection is plainer — a keyboard and a detent argue about the same 300pt.

Rejected: fullScreenCover. It buys 96pt of camera and costs the two things the flow needs most: the Mac's name in a title bar, and a success that pushes rather than dismisses-then-pushes. Do not hide the navigation bar over the viewfinder either. It is translucent, it is where the reader looks to confirm which Mac they are pointing at, and the title is the only place that fact appears once the camera fills the screen.

The wrong-QR state is a line, not an interruption. An alert would stop the camera and demand a tap for something that recurs every time the phone drifts over a sticker on the lid. So the hint line under the reticle is replaced by a capsule for two seconds — “That is not a Granita pairing code.” — throttled to one appearance per two seconds, and the scanner never stops. Note it does not say which code it found: nothing on the phone should imply it read a stranger's QR back to them.

One behaviour to build with the spend. The moment a Granita link is found, the camera session stops, the preview dims and freezes, and the back button is disabled until the outcome lands. A code that works once must not be abandonable halfway by an edge swipe — and freezing the frame is also the only acknowledgement the reader gets that the phone saw the code at all.

Call 2 · the countdown, in full
The phone learns a code exists at the instant it reads it, and granita://pair?host=&port=&code=&spki= carries no mint time. So a phone-side 2:00 can be up to 120 seconds wrong, and it is wrong in the dangerous direction: it reads 1:47 while the true remaining is nil, and the reader trusts it.

There is also nothing to decide with the number. Expiry and a code that never existed produce the same sentence and the same remedy — get a new code — so a countdown cannot change what anybody does. The Mac's countdown is different: the Mac minted the code, so its timer is true, and it is standing in front of the person.

If you want a clock on the phone anyway, the honest version is not a countdown but a consequence: after a refusal, the words screen keeps what was typed and says the code is stale. That is the same information, arriving when it is actionable.

Should feel like the Wi-Fi QR scanner in Camera: you point, it recognises, and the recognition is the whole event. Nothing pulses, nothing counts, nothing asks you to confirm what you just aimed at.

3.3  ·  THE FIELD

One field, and an echo in the Mac's own format

Recommendation. A single TextField, monospaced at body size to match the Mac's 13pt mono, with .textInputAutocapitalization(.never), .autocorrectionDisabled(), focus on appear, and .submitLabel(.go). Beneath it, two lines that do the actual work: the words the phone recognised, joined by middle dots exactly as the Mac joins them, and a count. The reader's eye can then compare the phone's line with the Mac's line character for character, which is a different and easier task than proofreading their own typing.

The field must not correct anything. Your server lowercases and accepts spaces, hyphens and middle dots, so the field has nothing to fix — and the capitalised first word you predicted is not an error to be caught, it is a keystroke the normaliser eats. Two settings matter more than they look: autocorrect will happily turn a word from a 128-word list into an English word that is not in it, and smart punctuation turns a typed hyphen into an en dash. If your normaliser does not accept en dashes, that is a two-character fix in the parser and I would do it there as well as here, because the reader can also paste.

The unknown-word line needs the word list on the phone. It is the one thing here you cannot build today: flagging “branch” is not one of the words means the client ships the same 128 words the Mac uses. It is worth it — it stops a wasted round trip, and a wasted round trip costs more than it looks now that five failures in a minute lock the address out. If you would rather not duplicate the list, delete that line and keep the count; nothing else in the screen changes.

Rejected: six boxes. Six auto-advancing fields is the shape people copy from SMS codes, and it is wrong twice here. It is not a stock control, so it would be the one hand-built thing in the app; and it makes a phrase into a form. Words are variable-length, the reader is reading them in a line, and paste — which is how the same-device case will really be solved — has to land in one place.

Also rejected: a word-by-word suggestion bar. Autocompleting from the 128 words would be quick to build and it would be a small oracle: it tells anyone holding the phone which words are candidates. The list is public, so this leaks nothing about the code — but it is the kind of convenience that ends up filling in words the reader never read, and a phrase they did not verify is a phrase they cannot correct.

3.4  ·  OUTCOMES, AND CALL 4

One screen, and a button only where the phone can act

Recommendation. One destination for a spent credential, five appearances of it. Three carry no action, and that is the whole reason the other two are believable: Try Again re-runs the health probe and the spend, Open TestFlight leaves the app. The two contract states say “the code was not used” because your handshake refuses before spending, and the reader cannot know that from anywhere else — it is the difference between walking back to the Mac and just tapping again.

Call 4: straight in, and you are right. No confirmation screen. On success the phone plays a .success notification haptic and the stack replaces the pairing screens with the worktree list, titled with the Mac's name. A silent success reads as nothing having happened when the destination resembles the origin; here a frozen viewfinder becomes a populated list, which is the loudest thing that has happened in the app so far. The haptic is not decoration — it is what tells the reader who looked away that the phone finished. And the replacement matters: back must return to the Mac list, never to a scanner holding a spent code.

Why g gets its own screen. Every other failure says “try again here”. This one says “go and delete something on the Mac”, and it is the only screen in the app that asks the reader to repair the other machine. It also has to state a fact no other screen states — the Mac now believes this iPhone is paired — because without it, the advice sounds like superstition. Two behaviours belong with it: the phone must discard the token it could not store rather than keep it in memory and pretend, and the screen must not offer Pair Again, which would leave a second device record beside the orphan.

The one condition that would earn g a button. If the use case can hold the token in memory and retry only the Keychain write, then Try Again becomes this screen's primary action and the trip to the Mac drops to the second sentence — worth it, because the common cause of errSecInteractionNotAllowed is transient. I have drawn it without the button because I do not know that the token survives that far, and a button that cannot work is the defect you named. Tell me it can and this screen changes by one control.

Rejected. Alerts for all of these — an alert would dismiss to a live viewfinder that immediately re-reads the same dead QR, which is a loop. Also rejected: a “Contact support” or bug-report action on g; the reader is the developer, and the selectable OSStatus is the whole bug report.

A premise to check before the rate-limit copy ships. Your §3.4 e says “from this address”, and the Mac review found the limiter keyed on request.head.authority — the address the phone dialled, which is the same string for every device. If that is still true, five failures from anywhere lock out everyone, and the copy must stay device-neutral, as drawn. If you key it per peer, the sentence can say “from this iPhone” and be kinder.

Should feel like a receipt. It tells you what happened, whether it cost you anything, and the one thing left to do.

Section six · where a premise broke

The six words carry no key

This is the finding I would most like to be wrong about, and it is the only place in the brief where a screen cannot carry the difference. Settle it before the words screen is built, because the answer could change call 1.

You wrote that the two credentials redeem the same pairing, and that the spki in the link is a security boundary rather than a convenience. Both are true, and together they mean the credentials are not peers. The QR carries the fingerprint over a channel nobody on the network can write to: your Mac's own screen. The six words carry a code and nothing else — no host, no port, no key — and the host and port they borrow come from a Bonjour record any device on the LAN can publish.

So the words path has to pin the certificate it is handed on first contact. That is trust on first use: whoever answers for MacBook-Pro.local at that moment becomes the pinned Mac, and a device that answers first learns the code the instant the phone sends it. The QR path has no such window, which is precisely what the spki parameter is for.

What I am not recommending: putting the spki in the TXT record. It would fix the already-paired state and it would look like it fixes this, and it does not. A TXT record is in-band — an impostor advertises its own key alongside its own host, and the phone pins exactly what the attacker chose. One field, two features, and only one of them real. Add the instance identifier for the paired-row work if you want it; do not let the key ride along.

What I recommend. Keep the six words exactly as drawn, accept trust on first use for that path, and let the design carry the asymmetry in two cheap places instead of one expensive one. First, the camera is ordered above the words on the entry screen — which is call 1, and this is its real argument. Second, one caption at the bottom of the six-word screen, .caption2, tertiary, no icon and no box: “The QR code also carries your Mac's key. Typed words trust the Mac that answers, so use them on a network you trust.” That is a true sentence about a pin, not the plaintext warning you retired — the connection is TLS either way.

Rejected: a fingerprint comparison. The Mac shows four characters of the key beside the words, the phone shows what it saw, the reader confirms. It is the SSH ceremony, it is buildable, and I would not ship it. It puts a security decision in front of the one reader who has already been pushed onto the harder path, at the moment they are squinting across a room — and comparisons like that get approved, not performed. A check nobody performs is worse than an honest sentence, because it launders the risk.

And if the client refuses to pair without an spki today, then the six-word screen cannot ship at all, the permission-refused state has no way out, and camera-first stops being a preference and becomes the only path. That is why this is the first thing to answer.

What I need from you, in order
1. What does the words path pin? Trust on first use, or does it refuse without an spki? Blocks §3.3 and could overturn call 1.
2. Can the phone ship the 128 words? If not, the unknown-word line goes and the count stays.
3. Can the pairing use case retry only the Keychain write? If yes, state g gets a primary action.
4. Is the rate limiter keyed per device or per dialled address? The sentence differs.
5. Does a TestFlight URL open reliably on a tester's device? Otherwise 3.4 c loses its button.
6. Will the token store keep a pairedAt date? One field, one better sentence in 3.1 b.
7. Does the normaliser accept an en dash? iOS smart punctuation will produce one from a typed hyphen.
Not drawn, on purpose
No countdown, anywhere on the phone. No manual address entry. No confirmation screen after success. No “Recent” or “Other Macs” in discovery, no Mac-side approval surface, and nothing from v2.
Two premises that did not survive the drawing

The scanner cannot be the first screen. §5's first call assumes the choice is camera or words. Drawn in sequence, the entry screen turns out to exist for a third reason neither option covers: at the moment the reader taps a Mac, the Mac is not showing a code yet, and somebody has to say so.

“Already paired” is a discovery problem wearing a pairing screen. §3.1 b asks for the state and it draws fine — but if the record change lands, the right behaviour is a row that goes straight to the worktrees, and this state shrinks to the case where the Mac revoked the token. Build the row; keep the screen for that one case.
